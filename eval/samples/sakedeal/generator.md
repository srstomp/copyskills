# Warm or Cold? Kimoto, Yamahai and Sokujo Sake
Nov 17, 2026 · @Menno · sakedeal.com
## English: Warm or cold? Kimoto, yamahai and sokujo sake
By Menno Tol, master sake sommelier
At almost every tasting I lead, someone asks the same question: should this sake be served warm or cold? The answer starts long before the sake reaches your glass. It begins with the shubo (also called moto), the yeast starter that every sake is built on. In this article I explain the three main starter methods, kimoto, yamahai and sokujo, and what they mean for the temperature you serve at.
### Why the starter matters
Before the main fermentation, the brewer makes a small, concentrated starter of steamed rice, koji, water and yeast. It has to be acidic enough to keep unwanted bacteria out while the yeast multiplies. How the brewer gets that lactic acid shapes the character of the finished sake.
### Kimoto: the traditional method
Kimoto (生酛) is the original method, refined during the Edo period. Brewers grind rice, koji and water into a paste with long wooden poles, a heavy job called yamaoroshi. Lactic acid bacteria that live naturally in the brewery then slowly acidify the starter. The whole process takes about four weeks.
The result is depth and structure. Kimoto sake usually has firm acidity, plenty of umami and an earthy, sometimes lactic richness.
### Yamahai: natural acid without the poles
In 1909, researchers found that the enzymes in koji dissolve the rice on their own, so the pole work could be skipped. This became yamahai (山廃), short for yamaoroshi haishi moto, or "starter without the yamaoroshi". The lactic acid still develops naturally, again over about four weeks.
Yamahai is often a little wilder than kimoto: savoury, gamey, high in acidity and amino acids, sometimes with a pleasantly funky edge.
### Sokujo: the fast modern method
Sokujo (速醸) means "quick brewing". Since about 1910, brewers have added commercially produced lactic acid right at the start. The starter is ready in about two weeks, and the process is very reliable. Today roughly 90 percent of all sake is made this way.
Sokujo gives a cleaner, lighter and more precise base, with less acidity and umami.
