# AIDA: Attention-Interest-Desire-Action

**Full name:** Attention-Interest-Desire-Action
**Best for:** Landing pages, ads, general marketing copy
**Default status:** Use AIDA when nothing else fits better. It works across almost every format and audience type.

---

## Framework Steps

### 1. Attention

Stop the reader. The first line, headline, or subject line must create enough disruption that the reader pauses. Attention is earned through relevance, surprise, or a bold claim -- not through volume or exclamation marks.

A reader who has not stopped is a reader you have already lost. Attention mechanisms that work: naming the reader's specific situation, making a counterintuitive claim, leading with a number that reframes the problem.

Attention mechanisms that fail: generic openings ("Are you looking to grow your business?"), corporate tone, and anything that could apply to any reader in any situation.

### 2. Interest

Hold the reader once you have them. Build interest by expanding the attention hook with information that feels personally relevant. This is where you earn the right to keep talking.

Interest is not entertainment. It is the feeling that this copy has something to say that matters to you specifically. Stakes, context, and specificity increase interest. Generic claims kill it.

The transition from Attention to Interest should feel like a natural deepening, not a subject change. Whatever opened the copy, this section makes it more specific and more relevant.

### 3. Desire

Shift from information to want. Desire comes from helping the reader see themselves using the product or experiencing the outcome. Concrete imagery, social proof, and benefit language move readers from "this is interesting" to "I want this."

Features become desire-triggers only when connected to outcomes the reader cares about. "14-hour battery" is a feature. "Work through a full conference day without looking for a plug" is desire.

Desire is built through specificity, not enthusiasm. Enthusiastic copy signals the writer's excitement. Specific copy creates the reader's excitement.

### 4. Action

Ask clearly. The CTA should name the exact step, reduce friction, and create enough urgency to act now rather than later.

One action per CTA. Offering multiple options at the action stage introduces decision friction that reduces conversion. Pick the single most important next step.

Vague CTAs ("learn more," "click here") waste all the persuasion work done in the first three steps. The action should feel like the logical conclusion of everything that came before it.

## Examples

### Example 1 -- SaaS: project management tool (landing page hero)

**Attention:** "Your team is not slow. Your tools are."

**Interest:** Most project tools add overhead instead of removing it. Standup notes get buried. Deadlines slip. Nobody knows who owns what. The problem is visibility, not effort.

**Desire:** Basecamp users report cutting weekly status meetings from four hours to thirty minutes. One dashboard, every project, always current. Your team works the same way -- the noise disappears.

**Action:** Start a free 30-day trial. No credit card required.

---

### Example 2 -- E-commerce: premium running shoes (product ad)

**Attention:** "Shin splints are not bad luck."

**Interest:** Most road shoes sacrifice cushioning for weight savings. That tradeoff punishes runners logging 30+ miles a week with inflammation that compounds over months, not days.

**Desire:** The Strato Pro uses a three-layer midsole that returns 15% more energy per stride while absorbing 40% more impact force at the heel. Tested across 500 miles before it shipped. Rated highest for long-run comfort by Running Warehouse reviewers three years running.

**Action:** Order now and get free two-day shipping on orders over $75.

---

### Example 3 -- B2B services: recruitment agency (cold email)

**Attention:** "You have had three engineering roles open for over 90 days."

**Interest:** Long vacancies compound. Each month a senior role stays open, team velocity drops and your existing engineers absorb unplanned scope. The cost is not just the salary you are not paying -- it is the delayed roadmap and the retained engineers quietly recalibrating their workload.

**Desire:** We fill senior engineering roles in an average of 23 days. Our pipeline is pre-vetted: every candidate has cleared a technical screen and a culture conversation before you see their name. You review three candidates, not thirty.

**Action:** Reply to this email to see three candidates matching your open React role. No commitment required.

## Common Mistakes

- **Starting with features instead of attention.** Listing product specs before earning the reader's interest. The reader does not care about features until they care about the problem.

- **Weak transitions between steps.** Jumping from attention to action without building interest and desire. Each step must earn the next. If your Attention is about email deliverability, your Interest should deepen that topic -- not pivot to something else.

- **Underpowered CTA.** Ending with "learn more" after doing real persuasion work. The CTA should match the energy of the rest of the copy. A reader who has been persuaded through three strong steps should not land on a vague button.

- **Desire built on features, not outcomes.** Writing "16-hour battery life" instead of "Work through a full day and a long flight without hunting for a plug." Features describe the product. Desire describes the reader's future.

- **Repeating the attention hook in the interest section.** The interest step must add new information, not restate what you already said. Interest is an extension, not a repetition.

- **Attention that relies on shock instead of relevance.** Provocative openings that are not connected to the reader's actual situation earn attention for a moment and lose it immediately. Relevance holds longer than surprise.

---

## When NOT to Use

- **Technical documentation or onboarding copy.** When the reader is already a customer and wants instructions, AIDA's persuasion arc is unnecessary friction. Customers want information, not persuasion.

- **Educational content designed for slow consideration.** If the goal is to inform rather than convert, AIDA's urgency push can feel manipulative or premature. Consider QUEST, which paces information delivery without pushing toward a conversion at each step.

- **Re-engagement copy where trust has broken down.** If the reader left or churned, starting with an attention grab can feel tone-deaf. PAS, which opens with the reader's problem rather than a claim, is often more appropriate for audiences who have had a negative experience.
