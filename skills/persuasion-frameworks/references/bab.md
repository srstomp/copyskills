# BAB: Before-After-Bridge

**Full name:** Before-After-Bridge
**Best for:** Case studies, testimonials, transformation narratives
**Strength:** Shows the change. Works wherever the reader needs to believe that a different outcome is possible for them.

---

## Framework Steps

### 1. Before

Paint the reader's current reality in specific, relatable terms. This is the world as it exists before the product or service. The Before state should feel familiar -- not exaggerated, not dramatized. Specificity is what makes it believable.

The reader should think: "Yes, that is exactly what it is like." If they cannot recognize themselves in the Before state, the After state will not feel relevant to them.

Before states work best when they describe behaviors and situations, not just feelings. "Spending three hours on manual reporting before any analysis can start" is more recognizable than "feeling overwhelmed by data." Behavior-based Befores are harder to dismiss.

### 2. After

Describe the transformed state. Show what life looks like once the problem is solved. The After is not a list of features -- it is a picture of a different day, a different experience, a different feeling.

The gap between Before and After is what creates desire. Make the After vivid and concrete. Use the same specificity register as the Before state. If Before used numbers and behaviors, After should too.

The After should feel like an achievable version of the reader's life, not an aspirational fantasy. Credibility depends on the After feeling plausible, not just appealing.

### 3. Bridge

Explain how the reader gets from Before to After. The Bridge is where you introduce the product or service as the mechanism of transformation. Keep it credible: describe the key mechanism, not every feature.

The Bridge should feel like a logical explanation of how the Before became the After -- not a sales pitch inserted at the end. The narrative arc from Before to After makes the Bridge feel inevitable rather than promotional.

Keep the Bridge proportional to the Before and After. A long Bridge shifts the frame from narrative to product pitch, which can undermine the trust built by the story.

Close with a clear next step. The CTA should be proportional to the trust built through the story.

---

## Format Notes

BAB works at multiple lengths. In a short testimonial ad, each section can be one to two sentences. In a full case study, the Before might be two paragraphs, the After might include specific metrics, and the Bridge might walk through the implementation steps.

The ratio matters more than the absolute length. Aim for roughly equal weight across all three sections. A long Before followed by a short After creates imbalance -- the problem feels more real than the solution.

BAB can be written in first person (testimonial), third person (case study), or second person (addressing the reader directly). Second-person BAB is closest to PAS in structure and works well in marketing copy where the reader is the protagonist.

## Examples

### Example 1 -- E-commerce: standing desk brand (testimonial-style ad)

**Before:** Twelve months ago, I was hunching over a laptop at a kitchen table, back aching by noon, unable to concentrate past 2pm. I had tried a lumbar pillow and a monitor stand. Neither solved it.

**After:** I now get through a full eight-hour day without the mid-afternoon energy crash. My posture improved enough that my physiotherapist noticed at my next appointment without me saying anything. I am getting an extra productive hour each afternoon that I had written off.

**Bridge:** The Uplift V2 adjustable desk made the difference. I move between sitting and standing four to five times a day now without thinking about it -- the memory presets mean I just press a button. It took three minutes to set up and I have not touched the settings since. If your back is costing you hours of focus every week, this is worth looking at.

---

### Example 2 -- B2B SaaS: data pipeline tool (case study opening)

**Before:** The data team at Meridian Logistics was running 40+ manual exports a week. Analysts spent the first two hours of every morning pulling reports before any actual analysis could start. Errors in the export process meant at least one dashboard had to be rebuilt from scratch each week.

**After:** Today, every dashboard updates overnight. Analysts arrive to current, validated data. The team reallocated those 80 weekly hours to model building and has shipped three new forecasting tools in the past quarter.

**Bridge:** Meridian connected their warehouse platform, CRM, and logistics data to Airbyte in four days without custom engineering. Automated syncs run every night with built-in error handling. Here is how they scoped and executed the migration.

---

### Example 3 -- Services: executive coaching (landing page section)

**Before:** Most leaders who come to us are at a similar point: technically excellent at their work, stuck in a cycle of firefighting, unclear on how to delegate without losing quality standards, and privately questioning whether they are cut out for the role they spent years working toward.

**After:** Six months later, the pattern is consistent. They are running fewer meetings, owning fewer decisions, and building teams that execute without constant input. The work feels like strategy again, not triage. Several describe it as the first time in years they leave the office before 7pm without guilt.

**Bridge:** Our coaching engagement works on three specific things: decision frameworks that reduce the cognitive load of day-to-day choices, delegation systems that transfer accountability without abandoning standards, and a weekly accountability structure that keeps you honest about what actually matters at your level. Applications for Q3 cohorts open April 15.

## Common Mistakes

- **Before that is too generic.** Writing "you are overwhelmed and stressed" instead of describing a specific, recognizable situation. Generic Before states fail the recognition test -- the reader shrugs instead of nodding.

- **After that lists features instead of showing outcomes.** Describing the product instead of the transformed life. "You will have access to our dashboard" is not an After state. "You will know your numbers before your Monday morning call" is.

- **Bridge that skips credibility.** Jumping from a vivid Before/After to a vague "our product helps you get there." The Bridge should name the specific mechanism -- the thing that actually makes the transformation happen.

- **Mismatch in specificity between Before and After.** Using concrete numbers in the Before ("3 hours of manual reporting daily") and vague language in the After ("saves you time"). Both sides should be equally specific or the After feels unearned.

- **BAB used as a features list.** BAB is a narrative frame, not a product description. If you find yourself listing feature specs in the Bridge section, you have left the framework. Name the mechanism, not the feature set.

---

## When NOT to Use

- **Cold outreach where the reader does not know you.** BAB requires enough trust for the reader to follow you through a narrative arc. In cold outreach contexts, PAS is more direct and builds trust faster by opening with the reader's specific problem.

- **Technical documentation or feature-heavy products where the outcome is hard to picture.** If the transformation is abstract or highly variable by user context, BAB's visual "After" does not land credibly. Use FAB or ACCA to connect features to outcomes without requiring a transformation narrative.

- **Audiences in active pain who want relief immediately.** BAB asks readers to take a narrative journey. A reader who is urgently looking for a solution wants the answer fast. PAS, which names the problem immediately and moves to the solution, respects that urgency better.
