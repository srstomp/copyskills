# Conversion Principles

Copy-level principles for pages and emails designed to convert. Use this when scoring the Action dimension in the rubric, or when copy has a conversion goal (signup, purchase, download, reply).

---

## CTA Placement Rules

| Principle | Rule |
|-----------|------|
| Primary CTA position | Above the fold on desktop. Visible without scrolling. |
| CTA repetition | Repeat primary CTA after every major credibility section (social proof, feature explanation, pricing). |
| Single-focus pages | One primary CTA per page. Secondary CTAs must be visually subordinate. |
| Email CTAs | First CTA within 200-250 words. Repeat at the end. Never more than two distinct CTAs per email. |
| Proximity rule | CTA placement next to the objection it answers increases conversion. Place CTA near the proof point that earns it. |

**What kills CTA performance:**
- Burying the CTA after paragraphs of features
- Multiple equal-weight CTAs that split attention
- Vague CTA labels ("Click here", "Learn more", "Submit")
- CTA that doesn't match the offer described in the copy

---

## CTA Copy Patterns

**Verb-first:** Start with the action ("Start", "Get", "Book", "Try", "Download"). Never "Click here" or "Learn more" as a primary CTA.

**Benefit-completion pattern:** "{Verb} your {benefit}": "Start your free trial", "Get your report", "Book your demo".

**Specificity beats generic:** "Get the 12-point checklist" outperforms "Download now". "Start 14-day free trial" outperforms "Try for free".

**First-person CTA test:** "Start my free trial" vs "Start your free trial." First-person slightly outperforms in most tests. Worth A/B testing.

## Friction Reduction Patterns

Friction is anything that makes the reader hesitate before the action. Identify and reduce it.

**Objection neutralizers near CTA:**
- "No credit card required" (removes commitment fear)
- "Cancel anytime" (removes lock-in fear)
- "Free forever on the Starter plan" (removes financial risk)
- "Setup takes 4 minutes" (removes effort fear)
- "Your data stays yours" (removes privacy fear)

**Form length rules:**
- Email capture: name + email max. Every additional field reduces conversion 10-20%.
- Demo request: company + name + email + one qualifying question max.
- Purchase: pre-fill everything possible; never ask for information twice.

**Commitment ladder:**
Sequence micro-commitments before the big ask. A reader who clicks "See how it works" is more likely to click "Start trial" than one going cold from a landing page.

## Urgency Patterns

### Ethical urgency (use freely)

Real constraints the reader should know about:

- Cohort closes / event date ("Closes April 18")
- Actual inventory ("12 seats remaining in the April cohort")
- Price change coming ("Current pricing through March 31")
- Opportunity cost ("Every week you wait is a week your competitors aren't")
- Seasonal relevance ("Q4 campaigns start now for most teams")

### Manipulative urgency (avoid)

Fake or exaggerated constraints that train readers to distrust you:

- Countdown timers that reset when they expire
- "Only 3 left!" on a digital product
- "This offer expires tonight" repeated every week
- False FOMO without real consequence

**Test:** Would you be embarrassed if a reader discovered the "urgency" was staged? If yes, don't use it.

## Above-the-Fold Principles

The fold is the bottom edge of the visible screen before scrolling. Copy above the fold must do three things:

1. **Confirm relevance:** "I'm in the right place for my problem"
2. **State the primary benefit:** one sentence; no features
3. **Show the first CTA:** even if the reader won't click yet, it signals what the page is for

**Above-fold formula for landing pages:**
- H1: Specific benefit statement (not tagline)
- Subhead: Who it's for and how it works (one sentence)
- CTA: Primary action
- Social proof: Logo strip or single strong number

**What does not belong above the fold:**
- Long paragraphs explaining history or context
- Navigation-heavy headers that pull attention off the CTA
- Auto-playing video without a clear pause
- Multiple competing CTAs

## Social Proof Placement

Social proof works best near the moment of uncertainty. Place it strategically, not decoratively.

| Placement | When to use |
|-----------|-------------|
| Below the hero | Establish baseline credibility before the reader scrolls |
| Adjacent to pricing | Reduce purchase anxiety at the most friction-heavy moment |
| Adjacent to the primary CTA | Confirmation at the decision point |
| After objection section | Prove the objection has been overcome |
| Email P.S. | Last thing a scanner reads; last chance to build trust |

**Social proof hierarchy (strongest to weakest):**
1. Named customer quote with specific result and attribution (name, title, company)
2. Specific metric with attribution ("Relay users save 6 hours/week" from Figma)
3. Named customer quote without a specific result
4. Logo strip of recognized names
5. Aggregate number ("4,200 teams use Relay")
6. Anonymous quote
7. Star rating without context

Always lead with the strongest proof you have. Don't bury your best testimonial on a "testimonials" page nobody visits.

## Objection Handling Placement

Map the reader's likely objections and place the answers before the CTA, not after.

**Common objections by type:**

| Objection | Copy response | Placement |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| "It won't work for us" | Specific use case or industry example | Before pricing |
| "We can't afford it" | ROI framing, cost of not acting, or entry-level pricing | Adjacent to price |
| "It's too complex to implement" | Setup time claim, onboarding description | Before primary CTA |
| "We're not ready yet" | Low-commitment entry point | Near secondary CTA |
| "I don't trust you" | Social proof, guarantee, named customers | Adjacent to CTA |

**Order:** Agitate the objection briefly, then answer it with evidence. Don't just deny the objection; show why it doesn't apply.
