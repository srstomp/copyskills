# Cialdini's 6 Principles of Persuasion

From Robert Cialdini's _Influence_. These are the psychological levers that move people from passive to active. Use them deliberately, not as a checklist.

**Rule:** One strong principle applied well beats three weak ones stacked together.

---

## 1. Reciprocity

People feel obligated to return favors. Give something genuinely useful first.

**How to apply:**
- Lead with a free resource that delivers standalone value (not a teaser with a paywall behind it).
- Offer the useful tip or insight before asking for the action.
- Use "you keep this regardless" language in guarantees or samples.

**Example phrase:** "Here's the exact template we use for our own onboarding emails. Take it. Then if you want help implementing it, we're here."

**When to use:** Top of funnel, cold outreach, lead magnets, newsletters.

**When to avoid:** Transactional contexts where the exchange is already explicit (pricing pages, checkout). The reader knows they're paying; implied reciprocity reads as manipulation.

---

## 2. Commitment and Consistency

Once people take a small action or make a statement, they tend to follow through in larger ways. Consistency with prior decisions is a strong motivator.

**How to apply:**
- Start with a micro-commitment: a question they'll answer yes to, a small opt-in, a free trial.
- Reference their prior action in follow-up copy ("You signed up for the trial because...").
- Ask readers to identify themselves with a belief or behavior before presenting the offer.

**Example phrase:** "You already know cold email works. You're here because your current approach isn't converting. Let's fix that."

**When to use:** Email sequences (reference prior behavior), onboarding, re-engagement, upgrade prompts.

**When to avoid:** First-contact copy where there's no prior commitment to reference. Fabricating a commitment the reader hasn't made feels manipulative.

---

## 3. Social Proof

People look to others' choices and behaviors as signals of what to do, especially in uncertain situations.

**How to apply:**
- Use specific numbers, not vague claims: "4,200 teams" beats "thousands of users."
- Quote customers with their name, title, and company visible. Anonymous testimonials have low credibility.
- Show social proof near the moment of uncertainty (next to the CTA, next to the price, next to the risky decision).

**Example phrase:** "Sarah Chen, Head of Marketing at Lattice: 'We cut our email production time in half. The first campaign we ran with Relay outperformed our previous best by 31%.'"

**When to use:** Landing pages (near CTAs and price), email (when proposing something new), sales pages.

**When to avoid:** Contexts where the reader already trusts you heavily. Overloading trust signals can undermine confidence ("why are they trying so hard?").

---

## 4. Authority

People defer to credible experts. Demonstrated expertise moves faster than claimed expertise.

**How to apply:**
- Show credentials in context, not in a bio page nobody reads: "In 12 years writing launch emails, I've seen exactly two approaches that work..."
- Cite third-party validation: research, press mentions, industry recognition.
- Use specific, verifiable claims rather than self-description ("innovative leader" says nothing; "featured in Fast Company's 2025 Most Innovative Companies" says something).

**Example phrase:** "Harvard Business Review called this the most reliable predictor of cold email conversion. Here's how to use it."

**When to use:** Thought leadership, case studies, anything where the reader hasn't heard of you, technical audiences who scrutinize claims.

**When to avoid:** When the authority claim would feel performative or irrelevant to the audience. B2B tech buyers don't care about press mentions; they care about customer results.

---

## 5. Liking

People say yes to people they like. Likability comes from similarity, familiarity, and genuine warmth.

**How to apply:**
- Write in a voice that matches how the reader actually talks, not how a brand wants to be perceived.
- Use "we've been there" framing to signal shared experience with the reader's problem.
- Use the reader's language, not your internal terminology.

**Example phrase:** "We spent four years doing this the hard way before we figured it out. We built Relay so you don't have to."

**When to use:** Brand copy, newsletters, founder-voice emails, community-facing content.

**When to avoid:** Enterprise or highly formal contexts where personal warmth can undermine perceived professionalism.

---

## 6. Scarcity

People value what's rare or disappearing. Real scarcity moves; manufactured scarcity destroys trust.

**How to apply:**
- Use genuine constraints: actual seat limits, real deadlines, true limited availability.
- Be specific: "12 spots left" beats "limited availability."
- Explain why the scarcity exists. Unexplained limits feel fake; explained limits feel real.

**Example phrase:** "We cap each cohort at 30 because every participant gets a 1:1 strategy session with a senior strategist. 9 spots remain for the April cohort."

**When to use:** Launch campaigns, cohort-based products, event registrations, time-limited offers.

**When to avoid:** SaaS products with unlimited digital delivery (applying scarcity here reads as manipulative). Evergreen content. Any context where the scarcity claim is not real.

---

## Principles Interaction Guide

| Combination | Effect | Example context |
|-------------|--------|----------------|
| Authority + Social Proof | Strong credibility stack | Sales pages, case studies |
| Reciprocity + Commitment | Nurture sequence arc | Email welcome sequences |
| Scarcity + Social Proof | FOMO with evidence | Launch campaigns |
| Liking + Commitment | Community building | Newsletter, brand voice |
| Authority + Scarcity | Expert access feels rare | Cohort programs, consulting |
