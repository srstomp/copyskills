# Newsletters Reference

Formats, section types, and engagement patterns for newsletters and transactional emails.

---

## Newsletter Formats

### Curated Format

Best for: aggregating the best content in a niche. The writer's job is curation and commentary, not original reporting.

**Structure:**
1. Intro note (2-4 sentences) -- context for the week, a quick observation, or a hook for the issue's theme
2. 3-5 curated links, each with:
   - The link title (as an anchor)
   - 2-4 sentences of commentary that add perspective, not summary
3. Closing CTA -- reply, forward, click, subscribe

**What the commentary must do**: add a reason to care. "Here's a good article about X" is not commentary. "This article argues X, which contradicts the conventional wisdom that Y -- worth reading if you work in Z" is commentary.

**What the commentary must not do**: summarize the article. The link exists. Give them a reason to click it, then get out of the way.

---

### Original Content Format

Best for: thought leadership, subject-matter expertise, brand building through editorial voice.

**Structure:**
1. Hook (1-3 sentences) -- a question, a provocative claim, or a specific scene that pulls the reader into the piece
2. Main article body -- AIDA structure per section. Each section earns the next.
3. Sidebar items (optional) -- 1-3 shorter items (links, quick notes, one-liner observations)
4. Closing CTA -- tied to the article's conclusion, not tacked on

**AIDA applied to newsletter sections:**
- **Attention**: the hook. Something counterintuitive, specific, or high-stakes.
- **Interest**: the setup. Why this matters, why now, why to this reader.
- **Desire**: the body. The argument, the evidence, the insight.
- **Action**: the CTA. What should they do with this? Reply, share, try, read more.

---

### Hybrid Format

Best for: brands that have original perspective but also curate from the broader ecosystem. Most successful newsletters fall here.

**Structure:**
1. Original intro section -- 150-300 words. The editor's take on something in the world.
2. Curated section -- 3-5 links with commentary, same format as curated above
3. Original close -- 2-3 sentences that tie the issue together or tease what is coming next

The intro and close should be written by the same voice. The curation can be more functional. The seams between sections should not be visible.

---

## Section Types

| Section type | What it contains | When to use it |
|-------------|-----------------|----------------|
| Featured story | Long-form original piece, 200-500 words | When you have a strong take worth developing |
| Quick takes | 2-5 short observations (1-3 sentences each) | When you have breadth but not depth this week |
| Resource spotlight | One tool, framework, or reference, with a 50-100 word write-up | When something is genuinely useful to your audience |
| Community highlight | Reader reply, submission, or question, with your response | When reader engagement is high; builds community signal |
| Tease for next issue | 1-2 sentences previewing what is coming next week | Every issue, at the close |

Use 2-4 section types per issue. More than 4 creates a sense of clutter.

## Engagement Patterns

### Ask questions
End the featured story or the closing section with a direct question to the reader. Not rhetorical. An actual question you would be glad to receive an answer to.

"What's the most frustrating part of your hiring process right now? Reply and let me know -- I read everything."

Reader replies are the highest engagement signal for email. They also warm your sending domain, which improves deliverability.

### Use reader replies
When a reader sends a useful reply, with their permission, excerpt it in a future issue. Credit them by first name and company. This:
- Shows other readers that replies are read
- Creates social proof for the community
- Generates content without you having to write it

### Tease the next issue
Close every issue with one specific thing the next issue will cover. Not "stay tuned!" -- a specific topic or question.

"Next week: why the companies that nail onboarding also tend to have shorter sales cycles. I'll show you the connection."

The tease functions as a pre-subject-line for the next email. If it is good, the next issue gets opened before the subject line even registers.

## Transactional Email Notes

Transactional emails (receipts, confirmations, password resets, alerts) follow different rules from marketing email.

**Priority order: clarity, then speed, then brand.**

1. The first sentence must tell the subscriber exactly what this email is and what happened. No hooks. No build-up.
2. The key information (order number, confirmation code, action required) should be visible without scrolling.
3. Brand voice is welcome but never at the expense of clarity.
4. One CTA if any. Transactional emails are not the place for upsells unless the upsell is directly relevant (e.g., "Your order is confirmed. Add expedited shipping?" -- acceptable. "Your order is confirmed. Check out our summer sale" -- not acceptable).

**Subject lines for transactional**: clear and functional. "Your receipt from [Company]" beats "Something special just arrived."

**When transactional is also promotional**: if a receipt email includes a promo code or upsell, the transactional content must come first, fully complete, before any promotional content begins.
