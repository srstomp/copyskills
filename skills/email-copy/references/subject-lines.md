# Subject Lines Reference

Formula categories, templates, preview text patterns, and character guidelines.

## The Job of a Subject Line

The subject line has one job: get the email opened. Not inform. Not summarize. Get it opened.

Preview text has a second job: give the subscriber a second reason to open -- different information from the subject line, not a repetition of it.

---

## Character Length Guidelines by Email Type

| Email type | Subject line target | Notes |
|------------|-------------------|-------|
| Cold outreach | 30-40 characters | Shorter reads less like a blast. Under 40 is the goal. |
| Newsletter | 40-60 characters | Room for more personality and specificity. |
| Nurture/drip | 35-55 characters | Varies by position in sequence. Early emails: shorter. Later: more specific. |
| Welcome | 30-45 characters | Warm and direct. The subscriber is already engaged. |
| Re-engagement | 35-50 characters | Direct. Honest. This is not the place for cleverness. |
| Transactional | 30-50 characters | Clarity over cleverness. The subscriber needs to know what this email is. |

These are targets, not hard limits. A 62-character newsletter subject line that is specific and compelling beats a 45-character generic one.

**Mobile preview**: most email clients show 30-50 characters on mobile. The first 40 characters are the load-bearing ones.

## Formula Categories

### 1. Curiosity Gap

Opens a question the subscriber needs to read the email to answer. Works best when the gap is genuine, not manufactured.

**Templates:**
- "The [topic] thing most [role] get wrong"
- "What [known result] actually took"
- "Why [common belief] keeps failing"
- "The [number]-word answer to [common question]"

**Examples:**
- "The onboarding thing most SaaS companies get wrong"
- "What a 90-day ramp actually took"
- "Why 'hire for culture fit' keeps failing"
- "The two-sentence answer to churn"

**Avoid**: curiosity gaps that are too vague ("You won't believe this") or that promise more than the email delivers. The gap must close inside the email.

---

### 2. Benefit-Driven

States the outcome the subscriber gets from reading or acting. Most effective when the benefit is specific and tied to the subscriber's actual goal.

**Templates:**
- "How to [achieve outcome] without [trade-off]"
- "[Achieve outcome] in [timeframe]"
- "[Number] ways to [achieve outcome]"
- "The [adjective] way to [achieve outcome]"

**Examples:**
- "How to cut onboarding time without adding headcount"
- "Reduce churn in 30 days"
- "3 ways to shorten your sales cycle"
- "The fastest way to find product-market fit"

**Avoid**: generic benefits that could apply to anyone ("Grow your business"). Specificity is the differentiator.

---

### 3. Question

Asks the subscriber something they are likely already asking themselves. Creates identification -- "that's my problem."

**Templates:**
- "Are you [doing the wrong thing]?"
- "What would you do if [scenario]?"
- "Is [common assumption] actually true?"
- "How long does [painful thing] take you?"

**Examples:**
- "Are you hiring for the wrong traits?"
- "What would you do if your top engineer left tomorrow?"
- "Is 'move fast' actually slowing you down?"
- "How long does your interview process actually take?"

**Avoid**: questions that are too open-ended ("How's your business doing?") or that feel like survey questions rather than genuine prompts.

---

### 4. Number or List

Sets clear expectations and signals a scannable, structured read. Numbers outperform "some" or "several."

**Templates:**
- "[Number] [things] that [outcome]"
- "[Number] mistakes [audience] make with [topic]"
- "[Number] [topic] examples worth stealing"
- "[Number] questions to ask before [decision]"

**Examples:**
- "5 retention tactics that don't require a discount"
- "3 mistakes founders make with their first sales hire"
- "7 cold email openers worth stealing"
- "4 questions to ask before changing your pricing"

**Avoid**: inflated lists ("101 growth hacks"). Credibility scales inversely with list length for many audiences.

---

### 5. Urgency

Creates a reason to open now rather than later. Only works if the urgency is real. Manufactured urgency destroys trust.

**Templates:**
- "[Deadline]-based: "Last chance: [offer] ends [today/tonight/Friday]"
- Event-based: "[Event] is [X days] away -- are you ready?"
- Scarcity-based: "[Number] spots left for [thing]"
- Relevance window: "This only matters if you're [doing thing] right now"

**Examples:**
- "Last chance: Q2 pricing ends tonight"
- "Conference is 2 weeks out -- still time to prep"
- "6 seats left for October cohort"
- "This only matters if you're hiring engineers right now"

**Avoid**: fake urgency ("Limited time offer" with no actual limit). Subscribers notice. It erodes open rates over time.

---

### 6. Personalized

Uses the subscriber's name, company, role, behavior, or situation to signal this is not a blast. Most effective when the personalization is meaningful, not cosmetic.

**Templates:**
- Name + relevant topic: "[First name], the [topic] question"
- Behavior-triggered: "Since you [did thing], here's [relevant thing]"
- Role-aware: "For [role]: [specific topic]"
- Company-aware: "How teams like [company] handle [problem]"

**Examples:**
- "Sarah, the hiring question" (when paired with a relevant send)
- "Since you downloaded the guide, here's the next step"
- "For VPs of Engineering: interview load benchmarks"
- "How teams like Stripe handle onboarding at scale"

**Avoid**: over-personalization that reads as surveillance ("We noticed you clicked link 3 in email 7"). First name alone is not meaningful personalization.

## Preview Text Patterns

Preview text is the line that appears after the subject line in the inbox view. It is the second subject line.

**Rules:**
1. Do not repeat the subject line. Add new information.
2. Do not start with "In this email..." or "This week's newsletter..."
3. Do not leave it blank -- the email client will pull the first line of your email, which is often an unsubscribe link or preheader text.

**Patterns:**

| Subject line type | Preview text approach |
|------------------|----------------------|
| Curiosity gap | Answer the gap partially -- enough to pull them in |
| Benefit-driven | Add a secondary benefit or the mechanism |
| Question | Offer the beginning of an answer |
| Number/list | Name the most interesting item on the list |
| Urgency | Reinforce with the specific stakes |
| Personalized | Add context for why this is relevant to them specifically |

**Examples:**

Subject: "The onboarding thing most SaaS companies get wrong"
Preview: "It's not the activation rate. It's what happens on day 8."

Subject: "How to cut onboarding time without adding headcount"
Preview: "Three changes that made the biggest difference for our customers."

Subject: "5 retention tactics that don't require a discount"
Preview: "One of them takes about 10 minutes and we've seen it work twice this quarter."

## A/B Testing Approach

Test one variable at a time. Testing two variables produces uninterpretable results.

**Variables worth testing (in order of typical impact):**

1. **Topic/angle**: does this audience respond more to a problem frame or a solution frame?
2. **Length**: short (under 35 chars) vs. medium (40-55 chars)
3. **Personalization**: name personalization vs. no name
4. **Tone**: direct vs. curious vs. conversational
5. **Emoji**: with vs. without (test one emoji, not decoration)

**What to measure**: open rate is the right metric for subject line tests. Click rate is affected by the email body, not the subject line.

**Minimum sample size**: 500 recipients per variant before drawing conclusions. Below 500, variance dominates.

**Do not optimize forever**: pick a winner after 48-72 hours. Move on.
