## 1. The Core Problem: Generic Advice Is Not Thought Leadership

Most content labeled "thought leadership" is a listicle in a blazer. A confident headline, a few bullets, and no argument.

Real thought leadership makes a claim a reasonable person could disagree with. It takes a side and defends it with evidence, experience, or original reasoning.

The test: could this piece be published under someone else's name without changing a word? If yes, it is not thought leadership. It is content.

"5 ways to improve your team's communication" is not thought leadership.
"Asynchronous communication is making your remote team slower, not faster" is thought leadership. It stakes a position. It can be argued with.

Before drafting, complete this sentence: "I believe [X], and most people in this field believe [Y], and I am going to show you why that matters." If the sentence cannot be completed, the piece has no argument. Find the argument before writing.

## 2. Opinion-First Structure

State the position in paragraph 1. Do not build to it. Do not save it for the conclusion. Readers decide within the first 100 words whether to keep reading.

**Paragraph 1:** The specific claim. One to two sentences. No hedge.
> Example: "The biggest mistake B2B companies make with content marketing is treating thought leadership as a brand-awareness play. It is not. It is a sales tool, and if you are not measuring it as one, you are wasting your budget."

**Paragraphs 2-3:** The problem with the prevailing view. What most people believe and why that is wrong or incomplete.

**Body (3-5 sections):** Each section advances the argument. Use evidence: specific data, named examples, direct experience, cited research. Vague generalities ("many companies find that...") undermine credibility.

**Penultimate section:** The strongest counterargument, engaged honestly. Explain why your position holds despite it.

**Conclusion:** The opening claim restated with more precision. The reader has the evidence now. Do not summarize. Sharpen.

### What counts as evidence

Not all support is equal. In order of persuasive weight for thought leadership:

1. Direct personal experience with specific numbers ("our churn dropped from 8% to 3%")
2. Named case studies from identifiable organizations
3. Published research with source and year cited
4. Patterns observed across multiple cases ("in every company I have audited...")
5. Logical deduction from accepted premises

Vague generalities ("research shows...", "many leaders find...") have no weight. They signal that the author is hedging rather than standing behind the claim.

## 3. Authority Building

Authority comes from specificity, not credentials.

Do not: "As a 15-year veteran of the marketing industry, I have seen many trends come and go."
Do: "I have run content programs at three B2B companies with over $50M ARR. Every one made the same structural mistake."

When grounding an argument in experience: name the specific situation, state what happened, state what you concluded.

> Example: "When we cut publishing frequency from five posts per week to two, organic traffic dropped 8% in month one and recovered by month three. Engagement on individual posts increased 340%."

When citing external evidence: name the source specifically ("a 2024 Gartner study", "HubSpot's State of Marketing report"), give the finding, and apply it to your argument. Do not drop data and walk away.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

A contrarian take is not reflexive disagreement. It is a position that differs from the mainstream view because the evidence supports a different conclusion.

Structure of a well-executed contrarian take:
1. Name the prevailing belief clearly
2. Acknowledge why people believe it
3. Introduce the complication
4. State your position with specific support

> Example: "Everyone in SaaS says onboarding should be frictionless. Remove every barrier, reduce time-to-value to seconds. That advice is right for consumer apps and catastrophically wrong for complex enterprise software. The friction is the onboarding. Customers who skip it churn in month three."

What kills a contrarian take: vague disagreement, no evidence, contrarianism for its own sake with no practical implication.

## 5. POV Development and Expert Positioning

A POV is the lens through which the author sees the problem. It is repeatable across pieces.

A strong POV: specific ("most communication problems are really incentive problems"), defensible, leads to non-obvious conclusions, applies across multiple situations.

To develop the POV: state the underlying belief as a principle ("X is actually Y"), test it against three different examples. If it holds across all three, it is a real POV. If it breaks in one, the argument is about a specific case, not a general principle.

Expert positioning is earned through argument quality, not asserted through writing. Patterns that build it: making a specific prediction with reasoning, sharing a mistake and what it revealed, disagreeing with a widely-cited framework and explaining the flaw. Patterns that undermine it: hedging every claim, attributing all knowledge to other experts, using complexity as a substitute for clarity.

## 6. Anti-Patterns to Avoid

**The listicle in disguise:** headline promises a thesis, body delivers disconnected bullets. Fix: find the one claim connecting the bullets and build the argument around it.

**The "it depends" conclusion:** states a complex question then refuses to answer it. Fix: make the call. Qualified conclusions ("In most B2B contexts, X outperforms Y, except when Z") are more useful than "it depends."

**The credential parade:** opens with roles, publications, and speaking gigs before making any argument. Fix: earn credibility through the argument. Credentials go in the bio.

**The balanced overview:** presents multiple perspectives without taking a side. Fix: a balanced overview is a research summary, not thought leadership. Take a position.

**The artificial urgency opener:** "In today's rapidly changing landscape..." Fix: delete the first two sentences. Most thought leadership pieces should start on sentence three.

**The conclusion that repeats the intro:** summarizes what was covered. Fix: restate the argument with more precision. The reader already read the post. Do not recap it.
