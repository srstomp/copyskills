## 1. Post Types

| Post type | Goal | Framework | Best for |
|-----------|------|-----------|---------|
| How-to | Teach a process | QUEST | Skills, tools, workflows |
| Listicle | Organize options or tips | AIDA | Comparisons, resources, rankings |
| Opinion | Argue a position | Star-Story-Solution | Thought leadership (light) |
| Comparison | Help readers decide | AIDA | Product vs. product, method vs. method |
| Ultimate guide | Comprehensive resource | QUEST | High-value SEO targets, cornerstone content |

Do not write a "listicle" when you mean an opinion piece. The generic "7 tips for X" format is often a disguised opinion piece stripped of its argument. Choose the post type that matches the actual goal.

## 2. Hook Patterns for Opening Paragraphs

The opening paragraph determines whether readers stay. It must create tension, curiosity, or immediate relevance. Lead with what the reader is already thinking or experiencing.

**Pattern 1: Problem-first hook**
State the problem before offering anything. Drop readers into the pain.

> Example: "You've written the blog post. You've published it. You've shared it on LinkedIn. Six weeks later: 23 views, 0 comments, and a quiet dread that you're doing something wrong."

**Pattern 2: Contrarian opener**
Challenge a belief the reader holds before explaining why.

> Example: "Most SEO advice tells you to write for the algorithm first, humans second. That's backwards, and it's why most SEO content reads like a robot wrote it."

**Pattern 3: Specific scenario**
Drop readers into a concrete moment rather than a generalization.

> Example: "Sarah runs a 12-person SaaS company. Her team publishes two blog posts a week. Last quarter, three posts drove 90% of their organic traffic. She has no idea why."

**Pattern 4: Data-led hook**
Lead with a number that reframes the problem.

> Example: "The average blog post gets 91% of its lifetime traffic in the first 30 days. After that, it falls off a cliff unless it was built to compound."

Avoid: vague scene-setting ("In today's digital world..."), self-referential openings ("In this post, we'll explore..."), and rhetorical questions that don't contain tension ("Have you ever wondered...?").

## 3. Section Structures by Post Type

### How-To Post

Structure: intro hook + context + numbered steps + troubleshooting + CTA

- Intro: establish the problem and promise the solution in 2-3 sentences
- Context: explain what the reader needs before starting (tools, prerequisites)
- Steps: numbered, action-led ("Click X", "Open Y", "Enter Z"), each self-contained
- Troubleshooting: address the 2-3 most common failure points
- CTA: one clear next action tied to the post topic

> Example structure for "How to write a cold email that gets replies":
> - Intro: most cold emails fail because they lead with the sender
> - Context: what you need (a real target, a specific hook, a clear ask)
> - Step 1: Research one specific thing about your recipient
> - Step 2: Lead with what you observed, not what you want
> - Step 3: Make one ask, not three
> - Troubleshooting: what to do when the first draft sounds like a template
> - CTA: Download the cold email swipe file

### Listicle

Structure: intro hook + 5-10 numbered items + closing synthesis + CTA

- Intro: frame why this list matters and what problem it solves
- Each item: headline + 2-4 sentences of explanation + 1 specific example
- Closing: synthesize what the list adds up to (not just "we covered X things")
- CTA: one next action

Do not pad the list. If you only have 6 strong items, write 6. "10 tips" with 4 filler tips is worse than "6 tips" that are all substantive.

### Opinion Post

Structure: thesis + supporting argument + counterargument addressed + conclusion restating thesis

- Opening paragraph: state the position clearly. Not a question. A claim.
- Body: 2-3 supporting arguments, each with a specific example or piece of evidence
- Counterargument: acknowledge the strongest opposing view and explain why you still hold your position
- Conclusion: restate the thesis with sharpened language, not repetition

> Example thesis: "Daily posting is destroying content quality on LinkedIn." Support it with specific engagement data, an observed pattern in declining post quality, and the economic incentive driving the behavior. Then address the counterargument that reach requires volume.

### Comparison Post

Structure: intro framing the decision + criteria explanation + side-by-side comparison + recommendation + CTA

- Intro: establish who this comparison is for and what decision it helps them make
- Criteria: explain the 3-5 dimensions you'll compare before comparing them
- Comparison: cover each criterion for both options; be specific, not vague
- Recommendation: make a clear call. "It depends" without further guidance fails the reader.
- CTA: point to the next step after the decision (trial, demo, purchase)

### Ultimate Guide

Structure: intro + table of contents + 5-8 major sections + conclusion + CTA

- Each major section functions as a standalone explainer with its own subheadings
- Include a table of contents at the top with anchor links
- Cover the topic with enough depth that readers bookmark it rather than searching again
- CTA at the end of each major section (light) and one primary CTA at the end of the guide

## 4. CTA Placement

### Mid-Post CTA

Place one contextual CTA at the natural inflection point in the post, typically after the core value has been delivered but before the conclusion.

- For how-to posts: after the last step, before troubleshooting
- For listicles: after item 5-6 (if 8-10 items total)
- For ultimate guides: at the end of each major section

Mid-post CTAs should be contextual, not generic. "Want to go deeper on this? Download the guide" outperforms "Subscribe to our newsletter."

### End-of-Post CTA

Always include one primary CTA at the end of the post. This is the conversion moment.

Rules for end CTAs:
- One CTA only. Multiple CTAs dilute action.
- Match the CTA to the reader's state of mind at post end: informed, warm, ready for next step
- Be specific: "Start your free trial" beats "Learn more"
- For educational content: offer a resource that extends the learning (template, checklist, course)
- For conversion content: point to the product, pricing, or demo

## 5. Internal Linking Guidance

Internal links serve two purposes: SEO (distributing link equity) and reader flow (keeping readers on-site).

### When to link

- Link to cornerstone content when you reference a concept covered deeply elsewhere
- Link to product or landing pages when the post is commercial-intent content
- Link to related posts at the end of the post ("You might also like...")
- Do not link every occurrence of a keyword. Link the first meaningful mention.

### How to write anchor text

- Descriptive: "our guide to cold email subject lines" not "click here"
- Natural: anchor text should read as part of the sentence, not a detour
- Varied: do not use the same anchor text for the same destination repeatedly
- 2-5 internal links per 1,000 words is a reasonable range; do not cluster them

## 6. Whitepaper Structure

Whitepapers follow ACCA. They are longer, more formal, and evidence-heavy compared to blog posts.

Standard whitepaper structure:

1. Executive summary (200-300 words): the problem, the key finding, the recommended action
2. Introduction: context, scope, and why this matters now
3. Problem definition: evidence that the problem is real and significant (data, case studies)
4. Analysis: your framework for understanding the problem or solution
5. Findings/recommendations: specific, actionable conclusions supported by evidence
6. Conclusion: restate the core argument and call to action
7. References or methodology (if applicable)

Tone: first-person singular is rare; passive voice is acceptable where objectivity matters; claims require citations. Length: 2,000-5,000 words. Longer is not more authoritative.
