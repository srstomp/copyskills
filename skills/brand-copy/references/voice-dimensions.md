# Voice Dimensions Reference

Use this file when drafting a voice profile. It provides the tone spectrums, personality mapping process, and example profiles to work from.

---

## Voice vs. Tone: The Critical Distinction

**Voice** is permanent. It is the brand's consistent identity, independent of context. A brand's voice does not change whether it is writing a case study, a tweet, or an error message.

**Tone** is contextual. It is how the voice adapts to the emotional register of a specific moment. The same brand voice sounds different when announcing a product launch vs. responding to a customer complaint. Both are valid. Neither breaks the voice.

Analogy: a person's voice is recognizable whether they are telling a joke or attending a funeral. Their tone shifts. Their voice does not.

When writing a voice profile, define the voice. Note tone variations separately. Do not confuse the two.

---

## Tone Spectrums

Every brand voice sits somewhere on each of these spectrums. Placing a brand precisely on each spectrum is the fastest path to a specific, usable voice profile.

| Spectrum | End 1 | End 2 | What it controls |
|----------|-------|-------|-----------------|
| Register | Formal | Casual | Vocabulary level, sentence structure, contractions |
| Gravity | Serious | Playful | Humor, lightness, willingness to be absurd |
| Complexity | Technical | Accessible | Jargon, assumed knowledge, explanatory depth |
| Posture | Respectful | Irreverent | How much the brand challenges, subverts, or pokes fun |
| Energy | Calm | Energetic | Pacing, punctuation, sentence length, exclamation |
| Warmth | Cool/Professional | Warm/Friendly | Personal address, empathy signals, relational language |
| Authority | Peer | Expert | How the brand positions relative to the reader |

A brand does not need to occupy a single point on each spectrum. Some brands are technically complex but casually delivered. Some are warm and also authoritative. The combination is the voice.

### How to place a brand on the spectrums

For each spectrum:
1. Read the brief for explicit signals ("we want to sound approachable but credible")
2. Analyze existing samples for implicit signals (contractions suggest casual; no contractions suggest formal)
3. Look at the audience: B2B enterprise tends toward formal/expert; consumer tends toward casual/peer
4. Look at the category: fintech tends toward calm/authoritative; consumer apps tend toward playful/warm

Place the brand at a specific point, not a range. "Slightly casual" is more useful than "casual to formal."

## Personality Trait Mapping

Choose 3-5 traits that together define the brand's character. The combination should be distinctive enough that a writer could make a word choice based on it.

Rules for choosing traits:
- Pick 3 at minimum, 5 at maximum. Fewer forces focus. More becomes diluted.
- Each trait should be observable in copy choices. "Bold" should produce different word choices than "cautious." If two traits produce the same copy behavior, cut one.
- At least one trait should create productive tension with another. "Precise and playful" is more interesting than "precise and thorough."
- Name the trait in a way that implies the behavior. "Knowledgeable but never condescending" is better than "knowledgeable." "Direct" is better than "honest."

Trait categories to draw from:

**Expertise signals:** knowledgeable, precise, rigorous, thorough, data-grounded, opinionated, no-nonsense

**Relationship signals:** warm, generous, inclusive, approachable, peer-like, conversational, human

**Energy signals:** energetic, urgent, calm, measured, confident, optimistic, playful, dry

**Posture signals:** bold, irreverent, challenging, respectful, humble, authoritative, subversive

## How to Define a Voice in 5-7 Attributes

A usable voice attribute has four components:

1. **Name** -- the trait in 1-4 words
2. **Definition** -- one sentence: what this means for how the brand writes
3. **This** -- a short example sentence that demonstrates the trait in action
4. **Not that** -- a short example sentence that shows what the brand avoids

The this/not-that pair is more valuable than the definition. Definitions describe a quality. This/not-that pairs constrain a behavior.

Template:
```
[Attribute name]
[One sentence definition of what this means]
This: [Example sentence]
Not that: [Example sentence showing what to avoid]
```

Number of attributes: 5-7 for a full voice profile. 3 is the minimum for a usable document.

## Example Voice Profiles

These are illustrative examples, not brand endorsements. Use them to calibrate what a usable voice profile looks like.

### Example A: Casual Expert (Mailchimp-style)

**Voice overview:** Smart, a little funny, always on the reader's side. The voice of the knowledgeable friend who works in marketing, not the consultant who charges by the hour.

**Tone spectrums:**
- Register: Casual
- Gravity: Playful (but not flippant when something matters)
- Complexity: Accessible (jargon-free; explains everything)
- Warmth: Warm
- Authority: Peer (knows more, but sits alongside the reader)

**Attributes:**

Plain-spoken
Avoids industry jargon even when the audience knows it. Chooses the common word over the technical one.
This: "Send your campaign when your list is ready."
Not that: "Execute your deployment cadence upon subscriber list optimization."

Self-aware
Willing to acknowledge product limitations or complexity without hedging. Does not pretend everything is easy.
This: "Setting up automations takes a little time to get right. Here's what to do first."
Not that: "Our intuitive automation builder makes complex workflows simple!"

Warm without being precious
Friendly and approachable, but does not use diminutives, corporate warmth phrases, or hollow affirmations.
This: "You're all set. Here's what happens next."
Not that: "Amazing! You've taken the first step on your email marketing journey!"

---

### Example B: Precise Professional (Stripe-style)

**Voice overview:** Technically authoritative, respects the reader's intelligence, and treats clarity as a form of respect. Never chatty. Never cold.

**Tone spectrums:**
- Register: Formal-leaning, but not stiff
- Gravity: Serious
- Complexity: Technical (assumes developer audience)
- Warmth: Cool to neutral
- Authority: Expert (and the reader expects that)

**Attributes:**

Precise
Uses the exact technical term. Does not substitute accessible language when it creates ambiguity.
This: "The webhook endpoint must return a 200 status code within 30 seconds."
Not that: "Make sure your server responds quickly so we know the message was received."

Direct
States the fact, then the implication. No preamble, no hedging, no throat-clearing.
This: "Card declines fall into two categories: hard and soft. Hard declines are permanent."
Not that: "When it comes to card processing, there are various scenarios you might encounter, some of which may be more difficult to resolve than others."

Respectful of the reader's time
Structures information so the reader can scan and skip. Does not bury the critical detail in a paragraph.
This: Callout box with: "Note: Test mode transactions do not affect live data."
Not that: Four sentences of explanation ending with the note buried at the end.

## Usability Test

A voice profile has no value if a writer cannot use it to make a decision. Give the voice profile and two draft sentences to a writer. If they cannot pick the one that fits, the profile is too vague.

Common failure modes:
- Attributes that are synonyms (precise and accurate produce identical copy behavior)
- This/not-that pairs where neither version would feel wrong for the brand
- Attributes that describe values rather than behaviors ("We value honesty" is not a voice attribute; "We say what competitors won't" is)
