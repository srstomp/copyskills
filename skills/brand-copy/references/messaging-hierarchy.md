# Messaging Hierarchy Reference

Use this file when drafting a messaging hierarchy, positioning statement, tagline, or elevator pitch. The hierarchy is a cascade: each level is derived from the level above it. Build top-down. Never start with the tagline.

---

## How the Cascade Works

The hierarchy is top-down. Each level derives from the one above. Build in this order or the tagline will feel invented rather than discovered.

```
Positioning Statement -> Brand Pillars (3-5) -> Proof Points (2-3 per pillar) -> Tagline -> Elevator Pitch
```

- **Positioning Statement:** Internal strategic claim. Never used verbatim in copy. Every other decision must be consistent with it.
- **Brand Pillars:** Themes that make the positioning credible. One pillar = one dimension of why the positioning is true.
- **Proof Points:** Evidence per pillar. Metrics, customer outcomes, product capabilities.
- **Tagline:** Sharpest compression of the positioning. Derived from pillars, never invented in isolation.
- **Elevator Pitch:** The spoken version of the full hierarchy.

---

## Positioning Statement

The positioning statement is an internal strategic document. It is not copy. It is the claim that all copy must be consistent with.

### Template

```
For [primary audience] who [need or problem],
[Brand name] is a [category or frame of reference]
that [key differentiator].
Unlike [primary alternative],
we [unique value or mechanism].
```

### What each element does

- **Primary audience:** The specific person, not a broad segment. "Founders of software companies with 5-50 employees" not "small businesses."
- **Need or problem:** What they struggle with, in their language.
- **Category:** Sets the comparison frame. "Project management tool" invites comparison to Asana. "Async collaboration platform" claims different territory.
- **Key differentiator:** What you do differently. Must be true, defensible, and specific.
- **Primary alternative:** What they use instead. Name it specifically: the spreadsheet, the legacy system, the workaround.
- **Unique value or mechanism:** Why you win against that alternative. Not "better" but "faster because X" or "cheaper because Y."

### Example

Brief: B2B SaaS for tracking freelancer time, targeting agencies tired of spreadsheets.

```
For agency operations leads who waste hours reconciling freelancer hours at month end,
Trackright is a time-tracking platform
that closes the reconciliation loop automatically.
Unlike spreadsheets and general-purpose tools,
we pull billable hours directly from the tools freelancers already use,
so there is nothing to manually compile.
```

## Brand Pillars

Brand pillars are the 3-5 recurring themes that make the positioning credible. Every major piece of copy should be traceable to at least one pillar.

### Rules for choosing pillars

- 3 pillars minimum. 5 maximum. Fewer forces clarity. More dilutes focus.
- Each pillar must be independently provable. If two pillars require the same proof points, combine them.
- Pillars are not features. They are claims about what the brand stands for that a feature can support.
- Each pillar should answer a different objection or reinforce a different dimension of the positioning.

### Pillar structure

```
[Pillar name: 2-5 words]
[One sentence describing what this pillar means for the customer]
Proof points:
- [Specific evidence: metric, behavior, customer outcome, product capability]
- [Specific evidence]
- [Specific evidence -- optional third]
```

### Example pillars (continuing the Trackright example)

**No manual data entry**
Freelancers do not fill out timesheets. Hours are pulled automatically from the tools they already use.
Proof points:
- Integrates with Toggl, Harvest, and Clockify (80% of freelancers already use one of these)
- Average agency saves 4 hours per billing cycle on reconciliation

**Accurate billing, first time**
Agencies bill on real hours, not estimates or rounded-up guesses.
Proof points:
- Automatic variance alerts when hours deviate from project budget
- Customer A reduced billing disputes by 60% in the first quarter

## Tagline Derivation

The tagline is the shortest true statement about the brand. It is not a slogan invented to sound clever. It is derived from the positioning and pillars.

### How to derive the tagline

1. Write the positioning statement.
2. Write the brand pillars.
3. Ask: what is the single most important thing a prospect needs to understand about this brand?
4. Find the shortest version of that thing that does not require context to understand.
5. Test it: does the tagline still work after reading the positioning? If it feels like a completely different claim, revise.

### Rules for taglines

- Under 8 words is the target. Under 6 is better.
- It must be specific enough to rule someone out. "Better together" could be any brand. "Hours tracked, automatically" is only for a time-tracking tool.
- Avoid verb-less taglines that sound like a category description. "Project management for agencies" is a category. A tagline makes a claim.
- A good tagline uses compression: it implies more than it says. The implication must be accurate.

### Examples

Weak: "Time tracking for agencies" (category description, not a claim)
Stronger: "Freelancer hours, no timesheets" (states the differentiator as a fact)
Stronger still: "Hours reconciled. No one asked." (implies the automation without explaining it)

## Elevator Pitch

The elevator pitch is the verbal version of the full hierarchy. It should be deliverable in 30-60 seconds, in natural spoken language, without slides.

### Structure

```
1. The problem (1-2 sentences): What the audience struggles with right now
2. The category (1 sentence): What kind of thing you are
3. The differentiator (1-2 sentences): What makes you different from alternatives
4. The proof (1-2 sentences): One specific, credible result or fact
5. The ask (1 sentence): What you want from the listener
```

### Rules

- Write to be spoken, not read. Long sentences that work on paper collapse when spoken aloud.
- Use the customer's language for the problem, not internal terminology.
- One specific proof, not a list. "Eight agencies cut their billing cycle in half" beats "our customers see better results."
- The ask matches context: "Could I send you a demo?" at a networking event; "What would that time savings mean for your team?" in a sales call.

### Example (Trackright)

"Agency ops is full of invisible work. At billing time, someone chases down freelancer timesheets, reconciles hours against budgets, and hopes the numbers are right. Hours of work, and the data is still wrong.

Trackright pulls hours automatically from whatever tools freelancers already use. No timesheets. The hours are there when you need them.

One customer cut their monthly reconciliation from 8 hours to 45 minutes. First month.

Would it make sense to walk through what that looks like for your team?"

## Common Mistakes

**Pillars that are features:** "Real-time dashboards" is a feature. "Clarity over what is actually happening" is a pillar.

**Positioning without a real alternative:** "Unlike other tools" is not an alternative. Name the actual thing: the spreadsheet, the legacy system, the workaround.

**Tagline invented in isolation:** A tagline that does not emerge from the hierarchy is a slogan. Slogans sound catchy in isolation and mean nothing in context.

**Proof points without numbers:** "Customers see better results" is not a proof point. "Customers cut billing time by 4 hours on average" is. If you do not have a number, use a specific behavior instead of a vague claim.
