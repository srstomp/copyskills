# Style Guide Template Reference

Use this file when drafting a brand style guide. The style guide is the most actionable anti-slop document available. A brand that defines "we say X, never Y" produces fundamentally different copy than default AI output. Spend the most effort on the do/don't section.

---

## Brand Style Guide Structure

A complete brand style guide has five sections:

1. **Do/Don't examples** -- This/not-that pairs showing how the brand writes vs. how it does not
2. **Word lists** -- Always use / never use / use sparingly
3. **Grammar preferences** -- Specific rules with defaults and exceptions
4. **Formatting conventions** -- Dates, numbers, product names, capitalization
5. **Voice summary** -- One-page distillation for quick reference (reference the voice profile for full detail)

Sections 1 and 2 are the most valuable. If time is limited, produce those two first.

---

## Do/Don't Examples

This is the most powerful section of any style guide. Abstract voice descriptions tell writers what to aim for. Do/don't examples show them exactly what to do.

### How to write effective this/not-that pairs

**Rules:**

1. Each pair should be about the same concept, delivered two different ways. The "do" and "don't" must be comparable alternatives, not random examples.
2. The "don't" must be something a writer would actually produce. If no one would write the "don't" version, the pair is not useful.
3. The contrast must be large enough to be instructive. Small stylistic differences are not worth the space.
4. Pairs should cover different failure modes: jargon, passive voice, vague claims, corporate warmth, excessive hedging, etc.

**Template:**
```
[Topic or failure mode]
Do: [The way this brand writes it]
Don't: [What to avoid]
Why: [Optional one-sentence explanation when the reason is not obvious]
```

### Example pairs (for a casual-expert brand like the Mailchimp archetype)

**Describing a product capability**
Do: "Pick the right time to send, without guessing. Our tool checks when your subscribers usually open email and schedules around that."
Don't: "Leverage our advanced send-time optimization feature to maximize open rates through machine learning-powered scheduling."
Why: Plain language over feature names. The benefit before the mechanism.

**Welcoming a new user**
Do: "You're in. Here's what to do first."
Don't: "Welcome to the platform! We're thrilled to have you join our community of successful marketers."
Why: Skip the hollow enthusiasm. Move the reader forward.

**Explaining an error**
Do: "We couldn't save your changes. Check your connection and try again."
Don't: "An unexpected error has occurred. Please contact support if the problem persists."
Why: Specific cause when possible. Next action always.

**Describing pricing**
Do: "Free for up to 500 contacts. Pay as you grow after that."
Don't: "Our flexible, scalable pricing model adapts to businesses of all sizes."
Why: The actual pricing beats a description of the pricing structure.

**Announcing a new feature**
Do: "You can now schedule posts directly from the dashboard. No more switching tabs."
Don't: "We're excited to announce the launch of our integrated scheduling functionality, which enables users to streamline their workflow."
Why: What changed, why it matters, that's it.

## Word Lists

### Always use (brand vocabulary)

Words and phrases the brand uses consistently that create a recognizable voice. These should feel slightly distinctive, not generic.

How to populate this list:
- Look for words that appear repeatedly in founder communications or existing strong copy
- Include product names, proprietary terms, and category language the brand owns
- Include plain-language alternatives to jargon terms that appear frequently in the industry

Example (for a B2B analytics tool):
- "data you can trust" (not "accurate data" or "reliable data")
- "your team" (not "users" or "stakeholders")
- "in practice" (signals real-world relevance, not theoretical)
- "the number" (singular, specific metric over a general discussion)

### Never use (banned vocabulary)

Words and phrases that violate the brand voice. This list should be specific enough to be surprising. Generic jargon lists are not useful. The words that this specific brand would never say are useful.

How to populate this list:
- Start with the anti-slop banned words list (leverage, seamless, robust, etc.) -- those are always banned
- Add category-specific terms the brand explicitly avoids because they signal the wrong positioning
- Add competitor vocabulary the brand does not want to mirror
- Add terms that have been used in previous copy that violated the voice

Example (continuing the analytics tool):
- leverage (and all synonyms: harness, empower, unlock)
- seamless (and: frictionless, smooth, effortless)
- "data-driven" (overused in the category to the point of meaning nothing)
- "actionable insights" (a phrase that promises meaning but delivers nothing)
- "decision-making" (prefer "making the call" or "deciding" -- shorter, more human)

### Use sparingly (contextual vocabulary)

Words and phrases that are acceptable in specific contexts but should not become defaults.

Example:
- "powerful" -- acceptable in feature comparisons, not in general descriptions
- "easy" -- acceptable when describing something genuinely simple, not as a default modifier
- technical jargon -- acceptable when writing for a technical audience, avoided in general copy
- exclamation points -- acceptable at product launch moments or genuine wins, not as enthusiasm padding

## Grammar Preferences

State each rule as a default, then note any exception condition.

| Rule | Default | Exception |
|------|---------|-----------|
| Contractions | Use throughout ("don't", "we'll", "you're") | Legal copy and formal compliance documents |
| Oxford comma | Always use ("Google, Meta, and LinkedIn") | None |
| Sentence fragments | Acceptable in CTAs, headings, pull quotes | Explanatory and instructional copy: complete sentences |
| Numbers | Spell out one through nine; numerals for 10+ | Percentages and measurements always numerals ("3%", "4px"); headings always numerals ("5 reasons") |
| Heading capitalization | Sentence case ("How to set up your account") | Product names and proper nouns |
| Em dashes | Never. Use a comma, period, or colon instead | None |
| Ampersands | Never in running prose. Write "and" | Short UI labels and navigation items |

**Note on capitalization:** Title case signals formality. Sentence case signals approachability. Match the brand's register.

## Formatting Conventions

Decide these once and apply consistently. Inconsistency signals no one is in charge of the voice.

- **Dates:** "April 4, 2026" for full dates. "Apr 4" in constrained spaces. Never "4/4/26" (ambiguous across locales).
- **Time:** 12-hour with lowercase am/pm. "9:00am", "2:30pm." Not "9:00 AM."
- **Percentages:** "34%" not "34 percent" in most contexts.
- **Product names:** List the exact canonical form. "Trackright" not "TrackRight" or "Track Right." Full name on first mention; short form acceptable after.
- **URLs:** No "www" unless required. Lowercase throughout.

## Voice Summary (Quick Reference)

One-page version for writers who need a fast orientation. This section references the full voice profile; it does not replace it.

Include:
- 3-5 voice attributes, each in one sentence
- Top 5 words to always use
- Top 5 words to never use
- Two do/don't pairs that capture the most common failure modes

This is the section a freelance writer should read before writing a single word for the brand. If it does not orient them effectively, the style guide has not done its job.
