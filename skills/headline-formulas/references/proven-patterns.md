# Proven Headline Patterns

Templates organized by type. Each has [bracket] slots and one concrete example.
Use these as skeletons. Swap your product, number, and audience detail in.

---

## Curiosity

Curiosity headlines withhold just enough to force the click. The gap between what the reader knows and what the headline implies is the tension that drives action. The answer must genuinely deliver.

**1. The Mistake Pattern**
`The [shocking number]% [audience] Who [do something common] Are [negative consequence they don't expect]`
> "The 73% of Founders Who Raise in January Are Setting Themselves Up for a Down Round"

**2. The Hidden Reason**
`Why [common belief] Is [actually wrong / the opposite of true]`
> "Why Posting Every Day Is Killing Your LinkedIn Reach"

**3. The Secret Pattern (Ogilvy-style)**
`The Secret [benefit] That [authority or source] Don't Want You to Know`
> "The Secret Pricing Strategy That SaaS CFOs Use to Hit 120% NRR"

**4. What They Don't Tell You**
`What Nobody Tells You About [common activity or milestone]`
> "What Nobody Tells You About Your First 90 Days as a VP of Sales"

**5. The Unexpected Correlation**
`The [surprising thing] That Predicts [desirable outcome] Every Time`
> "The One Email Metric That Predicts Churn Six Weeks Before It Happens"

---

## Benefit

Benefit headlines make a direct promise. Specificity is the difference between credible and generic. A number, a timeframe, or a named result makes it land. David Ogilvy: "The consumer isn't a moron. She's your wife."

**1. The Direct Benefit with Timeframe**
`[Verb] [specific outcome] in [timeframe]`
> "Write a Full Drip Sequence in 45 Minutes"

**2. The Result Without the Pain**
`[Desirable outcome] Without [the thing they hate doing]`
> "More Pipeline Without Cold Calling"

**3. The Comparison Lift**
`[Specific outcome] That [comparison benchmark] Can't Match`
> "Open Rates That Mailchimp Templates Can't Touch"

**4. The Before/After Compressed**
`From [undesirable state] to [desired state] in [timeframe or steps]`
> "From Zero Reviews to 50-Star Average in 30 Days"

**5. The Guarantee-Style Claim (Schwartz)**
`[Specific number] [audience members] Have Already [achieved result]. Here's How.`
> "4,200 Shopify Stores Have Already Cut Cart Abandonment in Half. Here's How."

## How-to

How-to headlines attract readers who are actively looking for the method, not just the outcome. They signal education and specificity. The more precise the method, the better the headline.

**1. The Simple How-to**
`How to [achieve result] [optional: without / even if / in timeframe]`
> "How to Write a Subject Line That Gets Opened Without Sounding Desperate"

**2. How to [result] Like [authority or aspirational model]**
`How to [achieve result] Like [specific reference audience trusts]`
> "How to Write Product Descriptions Like a Direct-Response Copywriter"

**3. The Step-Count How-to**
`How to [achieve result] in [N] Steps`
> "How to Onboard a New SaaS User in 3 Emails"

**4. The Conditional How-to**
`How to [achieve result] Even If [common objection or barrier]`
> "How to Build an Email List Even If You Have No Social Following"

---

## Number/List

Numbers signal specificity, scanability, and a promised payoff. Odd numbers perform better than even. The more specific the number (11 vs. 10), the more credible the claim.

**1. The Numbered List**
`[N] [Things/Ways/Reasons/Mistakes] That [outcome]`
> "9 Subject Line Mistakes That Tank Your Open Rate Before 8am"

**2. The Numbered Technique List**
`[N] [Copywriting/Email/Headline] Techniques [authority] Uses to [result]`
> "7 Techniques Eugene Schwartz Used to Write Bulletproof Headlines"

**3. The Comparison Number**
`[N] [Audience] Tested [Thing]. Here's What Worked.`
> "83 SaaS Teams Tested These Onboarding Emails. Here's What Worked."

**4. The Specific Percentage**
`[N] Ways to [action] and [specific result percentage or metric]`
> "5 Ways to Rewrite Your CTA Button and Add 23% to Your Conversion Rate"

## Question

Question headlines work when the reader already feels the problem. If they don't feel the pain, the question falls flat. The question should name the exact frustration, not the category of the frustration.

**1. The Pain Question**
`Still [struggling with specific problem]?`
> "Still Rewriting the Same Email Three Times Before You Hit Send?"

**2. The Diagnostic Question**
`Are You Making [common mistake] When You [do common activity]?`
> "Are You Making These Pricing Page Mistakes When You Launch a New Plan?"

**3. The Aspirational Question**
`What Would [desirable outcome] Mean for [your audience's real goal]?`
> "What Would a 40% Reply Rate Mean for Your Q3 Pipeline?"

**4. The Rhetorical Challenge (Sugarman)**
`Do You Have What It Takes to [aspirational activity]?`
> "Do You Have What It Takes to Write Copy That Closes in a Single Read?"

---

## Command

Command headlines work when the reader is primed to act. They perform well in ad copy, email subject lines, and short-form social. The verb is everything: pick one that creates immediate tension.

**1. The Direct Command**
`[Strong verb] [specific thing] [now / today / before X]`
> "Send This Email Before Your Trial Expires"

**2. The Stop Pattern**
`Stop [doing painful thing]. [Brief promise] instead.`
> "Stop Writing Four-Paragraph Cold Emails. Try This Instead."

**3. The Never/Always Pattern**
`Never [struggle with thing] Again`
> "Never Stare at a Blank Subject Line Again"

**4. The Imperative with Reason**
`[Verb] [action] Before [deadline or consequence]`
> "Book Your Demo Before Pricing Changes on May 1"

---

## Combination Notes

Most breakthrough headlines combine two types. Common pairings:

- Curiosity + Number: "The 3 Words That Made This Subject Line Our Best Performer"
- Benefit + How-to: "How to Double Your Reply Rate Without Changing Your Offer"
- Question + Command: "Still Getting Ghosted? Do This Instead."

A pattern is a scaffold. Specificity is the material. Generic slots produce generic headlines.
