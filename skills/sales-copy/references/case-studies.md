# Case Studies Reference

Structures, patterns, and examples for B2B customer case studies.

## The Core Principle

The customer is the hero. Your product is the tool they used to win.

A case study that centers on your product's features is a brochure. A case study that centers on a customer's transformation is a story. Stories get shared. Brochures get ignored.

---

## Structure

Every case study follows four movements: situation, challenge, solution, results. They map directly to the BAB framework (Before-After-Bridge).

### Situation (Before)
Set the scene. Who is the customer? What does their business do? What was the context before they engaged with you?

Keep this to 1-2 short paragraphs. The reader does not need the company's full history. They need enough context to recognize themselves in the customer.

- Name the company, its industry, and its relevant scale (company size, team size, transaction volume -- whatever is relevant to the problem)
- Name the stakes: what was happening in their world that made the problem matter

### Challenge (Before, continued)
Name the specific problem they were trying to solve. Be precise.

- What was breaking or missing?
- What had they already tried?
- What was the cost of the problem -- in time, money, headcount, or opportunity?

The challenge section is where vague language destroys credibility. "They were struggling with inefficiency" tells the reader nothing. "Their onboarding process required 14 manual handoffs and averaged 23 days" tells them exactly what was broken.

### Solution (Bridge)
Describe what they did. How did they implement the product or service? What changed in their process?

- Focus on the journey, not the feature list
- Name the specific capabilities they used and why those mattered in their context
- Include a customer quote here if you have one that speaks to the experience of change, not just the outcome

### Results (After)
This is the most important section. It is also where most case studies fail.

Results must be expressed in specific, measurable terms. See the Metric Presentation section below.

---

## Metric Presentation

Specific numbers are non-negotiable. Vague claims are invisible.

### The test
Before writing a result, ask: "Could a competitor paste their name over this case study and have it still apply?" If the answer is yes, the result is too generic.

"We helped Company X improve their onboarding process" -- any vendor could claim this.
"We helped Company X cut onboarding from 23 days to 4" -- this is attached to a specific situation.

### What good looks like
Express every meaningful result as a change between two specific states:

- "Reduced mean time to hire from 47 days to 12"
- "Cut customer support tickets by 64% (from 1,400/month to 500/month) in the first quarter"
- "Freed up 18 hours per week per rep that had been spent on manual data entry"
- "Increased pipeline conversion from 11% to 19% over 6 months"

### Patterns for numbers you do not have
If the brief does not include specific numbers, flag them for client verification. Do not substitute vague language. Options:

- Use a percentage range if you have a range: "30-40% reduction in support volume"
- Use a relative comparison: "Onboarding time dropped by two-thirds"
- Flag the gap explicitly: "[Client to confirm: reduction in time-to-hire]" -- better to have a visible placeholder than a falsely confident claim

### What to avoid
- "Significant improvement" -- not a number
- "Better results" -- not a result
- "Improved efficiency" -- efficiency in what, by how much?
- "Faster than before" -- faster by how much?
- "Saved time and money" -- how much time? how much money?

Every one of these phrases is a slop flag. Treat them as errors.

## Customer Quote Placement

Quotes serve three functions in a case study: credibility, voice, and emotional resonance. Place them where they do the most work.

### Where quotes land best

**After the challenge section**: a quote that confirms the problem was real and painful gives the reader permission to empathize.

"We had five people whose full-time job was reconciling data between systems. It was embarrassing." -- VP Operations, [Company]

**After the solution section**: a quote about the experience of change (not just the outcome) makes the transition feel real.

"The first week we ran it, I kept waiting for something to break. It didn't." -- Head of Engineering, [Company]

**At the end of results**: a forward-looking quote that signals the customer would make the same choice again closes the credibility loop.

### What makes a quote useful
- Specific enough to be unswappable (you could not put another company's name on it)
- Speaks to feeling, not just facts -- the reader remembers emotion
- Sounds like a human being said it, not like it was written for a press release

### What to avoid
- "We're really happy with the results." -- empty
- "The team at [Vendor] was great to work with." -- a testimonial, not a case study quote
- Quotes longer than three sentences -- they lose punch

## Common Failure Modes

**Vendor-as-hero structure**: the case study opens with a paragraph about the vendor's product. The customer becomes a supporting character. Reorder so the customer's situation comes first.

**The features tour**: the solution section reads like a product manual. ("They used Feature A, which allows users to do X. They also used Feature B, which does Y.") Replace with narrative: how did using these capabilities change what the customer did day to day?

**Unverified results**: numbers invented or estimated without client confirmation will eventually surface and damage trust. Flag every number that has not been confirmed.

**Missing conflict**: a case study with no real pain in the challenge section reads like a press release. The reader needs to believe the problem was real. Name the cost, the frustration, the thing that was broken.

## Example Outlines

### Example 1: SaaS -- HR Technology (Time-to-Hire)

**Company**: Mid-market logistics company, 800 employees, engineering-heavy workforce.

**Situation**: Competing for engineering talent in a tight market. Hiring manager bandwidth was the bottleneck.

**Challenge**: Mean time to hire for engineering roles was 47 days. Each open role consumed 6-8 hours per week from senior engineers in interviews. The process relied on spreadsheets and email threads; coordination between recruiters and hiring managers broke down regularly.

**Solution**: Implemented structured interview workflows and automated coordination. Senior engineers moved from unstructured interviews to scored rubrics. Recruiter-to-manager handoffs went from email to a shared queue.

**Results**:
- Mean time to hire dropped from 47 days to 12
- Interview load per senior engineer fell from 6-8 hours/week to under 2
- First-year retention rate for hires made through the new process was 91% vs. 74% under the old process

**Quote (end of solution)**: "My eng leads stopped complaining about hiring. That was the signal." -- VP Engineering

### Example 2: Professional Services -- Revenue Operations

**Company**: B2B SaaS company, Series B, 120-person sales and customer success team.

**Situation**: Rapid headcount growth had outpaced their revenue operations infrastructure. CRM data was inconsistent, forecasting was manual, and the RevOps team of three was spending most of their time on data cleanup.

**Challenge**: Sales forecasts were off by an average of 35% each quarter. The RevOps team ran 22 hours per week of manual reconciliation between CRM, billing, and finance systems. Leadership had lost confidence in the numbers and was making headcount decisions based on gut.

**Solution**: Rebuilt the data pipeline connecting CRM, billing, and financial reporting. Established data governance rules enforced at the point of entry. Eliminated the reconciliation step entirely.

**Results**:
- Forecast accuracy improved from 65% to 91% over two quarters
- RevOps team reclaimed 18 hours per week previously spent on data cleanup
- Board reporting cycle shortened from 3 days to 4 hours

**Quote (after challenge)**: "We were running a $30M business off a spreadsheet that three people were manually updating. Everyone knew it was wrong; no one knew by how much." -- CFO

---

### Example 3: Manufacturing -- Supply Chain

**Company**: Mid-size contract manufacturer, 400 employees, 3 production facilities.

**Situation**: Increased order variability post-pandemic had exposed gaps in their inventory planning system. They were running on a legacy ERP that required manual updates and had no real-time visibility across facilities.

**Challenge**: Stockouts were causing production line stoppages at an average of 2.3 incidents per week. Each stoppage cost an estimated $14,000 in lost production and expediting fees. Procurement decisions were based on 2-week-old data.

**Solution**: Deployed real-time inventory visibility across all three facilities. Automated reorder triggers replaced manual procurement review. Demand signals from customer orders fed directly into production scheduling.

**Results**:
- Production stoppages fell from 2.3 per week to 0.2 per week within 90 days
- Expediting costs dropped by $620,000 in the first year
- Inventory carrying costs reduced by 18% through more accurate reorder timing

**Quote (end of results)**: "We stopped losing Fridays to emergency procurement calls. That alone was worth it." -- VP Operations
