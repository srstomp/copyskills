# One-Pagers Reference

Structures, patterns, and examples for single-page sales documents.

## The Core Constraint

One page. One CTA. One decision.

A one-pager fails when it tries to be a brochure. Every element on the page competes for attention. The job is to make the reader want to take one specific next step -- not to tell them everything about the product.

Before drafting, identify the single action you want the reader to take. Every word either earns its place by moving the reader toward that action or it gets cut.

---

## Information Hierarchy

The reader's eye moves top to bottom, then left to right. Use that path deliberately.

### The standard hierarchy for single-page sales docs

**1. Headline (top, largest type)**
The value proposition in one sentence. Lead with the outcome the customer gets, not the name of the product or the category it belongs to.

- Strong: "Cut engineering onboarding from 3 weeks to 4 days"
- Weak: "The leading platform for engineering team onboarding"
- Weak: "Streamline your onboarding with AI-powered workflows"

The headline should be specific enough that a competitor could not swap in their name and use it.

**2. Subheadline or supporting line (directly below headline)**
One sentence that adds the "for whom" or "how" that the headline omits. Not a repetition of the headline. A complement.

- "Built for engineering teams scaling from 20 to 200."

**3. Three key benefits (middle section)**
Not features. Benefits. The difference matters.

A feature is what the product does. A benefit is what the customer gets as a result.

- Feature: "Automated task assignment"
- Benefit: "New hires reach full productivity in 4 days instead of 3 weeks"

Write three benefits, each in one sentence. Three is the right number for a one-pager. Fewer feels thin. More feels like a feature list.

If you have a number, use it. "Cut onboarding from 3 weeks to 4 days" is a benefit with proof baked in. "Faster onboarding" is a benefit with nothing behind it.

**4. Social proof (below benefits)**
One piece of evidence that the claim is real. Options in order of strength:

- A named customer outcome with a specific metric: "Acme Corp cut time-to-productivity by 78% in the first quarter"
- A recognizable logo or customer name with a short quote
- A stat from a defined customer base: "Avg. time-to-productivity across 47 customers: 4.2 days"
- A brief attribution quote from a named person with their title and company

Unnamed testimonials ("A Fortune 500 company said...") are the weakest form of social proof. Avoid when anything better is available.

**5. Single CTA (bottom, prominent)**
One button, one action, one destination.

The CTA should say what happens next, not what the product is.

- Strong: "Book a 20-minute walkthrough"
- Strong: "See the 5-minute demo"
- Weak: "Learn more"
- Weak: "Get started"
- Weak: "Contact us"

"Learn more" is not an action. "Book a 20-minute walkthrough" is a commitment with a known cost and a clear next step.

## What to Include vs. Cut

### Include
- Specific outcome numbers
- The customer segment this is built for (so readers self-select)
- One proof point (case study stat, customer name, or measurable result)
- One clearly labeled CTA

### Cut
- Company history or founding story
- Awards and certifications (unless the buyer segment specifically cares -- compliance buyers sometimes do)
- Feature lists longer than 3 items
- More than one CTA
- Anything that requires the reader to already understand your category

### The "so what?" test
Read each sentence and ask "so what?" If the answer is obvious, the sentence earns its place. If you have to explain why it matters, the sentence is not doing enough work. Either rewrite it so the "so what" is baked in, or cut it.

## Visual Balance with Copy

One-pagers are laid out documents, but the copy structure drives the layout. Write copy with layout in mind.

### Copy density
The total word count for a one-pager should land between 120 and 200 words (not counting headline and CTA). More than 200 words means the reader has to work. Under 120 words can feel thin unless the visual elements are carrying weight.

### Breathing room
Short sentences create visual whitespace even in dense layouts. Long sentences collapse it. Default to sentences under 15 words in the benefits section.

### Scannable structure
A reader should be able to get the headline, skim the three benefits, read the social proof, and see the CTA in under 10 seconds. If the structure does not support that scan, the layout is fighting the copy.

## Example One-Pager Descriptions

### Example 1: Engineering Onboarding Tool

**Target reader**: VP Engineering or Head of Engineering at a company scaling headcount

**Headline**: "New engineers ship their first PR in 4 days. Not 3 weeks."

**Subheadline**: "Onboarding automation for engineering teams scaling from 20 to 200."

**Three benefits**:
1. Automated setup means new hires hit their first milestone without waiting for IT tickets -- average time to first commit is 4.2 days across our customer base
2. Onboarding checklists adapt to role and stack, so a frontend engineer and a data engineer get different paths -- not a one-size spreadsheet
3. Managers get a live view of where each new hire is, without scheduling check-in calls

**Social proof**: "We went from a 3-week onboarding process to 4 days. Senior engineers stopped losing Fridays to setup calls." -- VP Engineering, Acme Corp

**CTA**: "See a 10-minute walkthrough"

---

### Example 2: Revenue Operations Platform

**Target reader**: CRO or VP Sales at a B2B company with a mid-market or enterprise motion

**Headline**: "Your CRM data is 3 weeks stale. Your forecasts show it."

**Subheadline**: "Real-time revenue intelligence for sales teams that need accurate numbers to make decisions."

**Three benefits**:
1. Forecast accuracy improves to 90%+ within two quarters -- no more end-of-quarter surprises that force re-planning
2. RevOps teams eliminate an average of 18 hours per week of manual reconciliation between CRM, billing, and finance
3. Pipeline hygiene enforced at point of entry, so managers stop chasing reps for updates

**Social proof**: "We went from missing our forecast by 35% every quarter to hitting within 5%. Our board stopped asking for the manual backup." -- CFO, Series B SaaS company (150 employees)

**CTA**: "Get a custom pipeline health report"

### Example 3: Competitive Battle Card Format

Battle cards are a variant of the one-pager designed for internal sales rep use, not external distribution. The structure differs:

**Header**: Competitor name and the deal situation where this card applies ("Use when prospect mentions [Competitor X]")

**Their positioning**: How the competitor describes themselves (1-2 sentences, in their own words where possible)

**Their real weaknesses**: 3 specific, defensible claims about where they fall short -- not generic ("they're slow", "they're expensive") but verifiable ("their implementation timeline averages 6-8 months based on G2 reviews; ours averages 6 weeks")

**Our differentiation**: 3 specific claims where you win, each tied to a proof point or customer evidence

**Objection responses**: 2-3 specific objections a rep is likely to hear when competing with this vendor, with a recommended response for each

**Landmines to plant**: 1-2 questions a rep can ask that expose the competitor's weaknesses without naming them directly ("How do they handle [X scenario]?")

Battle cards fail when the differentiation is aspirational ("we're more innovative") rather than verifiable ("our average implementation is 6 weeks; ask them for their average"). Reps cannot use claims they cannot defend.
