# Proposals Reference

Structures, patterns, and examples for B2B sales and project proposals.

## The Core Rule

Open with the client's problem. Not your company bio. Not your mission statement. Not your list of awards.

The most common failure in B2B proposals is a cover page and executive summary that spend 300 words on the vendor before mentioning the client once. The client is reading to find out if you understand their situation. If the first thing they read is about you, you have already signaled that you do not.

---

## Proposal Frameworks

Use ACCA (Awareness-Comprehension-Conviction-Action) as the backbone.

| Stage | What it does | Where it appears |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|
| Awareness | Name the problem clearly so the client recognizes it | Executive summary, Problem section |
| Comprehension | Show you understand the cause, context, and cost of the problem | Problem section, Situation analysis |
| Conviction | Build confidence that your solution will work for their situation | Solution section, Approach, Evidence |
| Action | Make the next step obvious and low-friction | Pricing, Timeline, Next steps |

Each section of the proposal should advance one of these stages. If a section does not move the reader from Awareness through to Action, it probably should not be in the proposal.

---

## Section Order

The section order below applies to most B2B proposals. Adjust based on RFP requirements or deal context, but do not move the executive summary or the problem framing.

1. **Executive summary** (1 page max)
2. **Situation and problem** (the client's context and what needs to change)
3. **Proposed solution and approach** (what you will do and how)
4. **Evidence and credentials** (relevant proof that this approach works)
5. **Scope and deliverables** (what is included and what is not)
6. **Timeline and milestones**
7. **Investment** (pricing)
8. **Next steps**

## Executive Summary Patterns

The executive summary is the one section every decision-maker will read. Treat it as the whole proposal compressed into one page.

### Structure
**Paragraph 1: The client's situation and problem**
Open with what you understand about their current state and what is at stake. Use specific details from discovery calls or the RFP. Do not open with "We are pleased to present this proposal."

**Paragraph 2: Your recommended solution**
One paragraph on what you are proposing. Focus on the outcome they will achieve, not the process you will run.

**Paragraph 3: Why this approach works for their situation**
One paragraph connecting your approach to their specific context. Reference relevant experience or evidence briefly.

**Paragraph 4: The ask**
What you are recommending they approve, and what happens next.

### What to avoid
- Opening with company history ("Founded in 2008, we have helped hundreds of clients...")
- Opening with a generic mission statement ("We are committed to delivering excellence...")
- Listing features or services before naming the problem
- Using "we" more than "you" in the first two paragraphs (count them)

### The "we/you" test
Count the instances of "we" and "you" in the executive summary. In a strong proposal, "you" outnumbers "we" in the first two paragraphs. If "we" dominates throughout, the proposal is vendor-centric. Rewrite until the client is the subject of most sentences.

## Pricing Presentation

### Anchor high, show value first
Present the investment section after the solution and evidence sections, not before. The reader should understand what they are buying and why it works before they see the number.

Anchoring works because the first number the reader encounters shapes how they interpret the price. If you name the cost of the problem before the cost of the solution, the solution looks inexpensive. ("Your current process is costing you an estimated $400K per year in inefficiency. Our implementation investment is $85K.")

### Show price in context
Present cost alongside outcome wherever possible:
- "Investment of $85K against an estimated $400K annual cost of the current process"
- "Engagement fee of $120K for a 6-month project that our comparable clients have seen generate $800K-$1.2M in new pipeline"

### Structure pricing tiers carefully
If offering multiple options, present three tiers: a baseline, a recommended option, and a premium option. The recommended option should be priced between the other two. Readers tend to avoid the extremes.

Label the tiers by outcome ("Growth Plan", "Scale Plan", "Enterprise Plan"), not by features ("Basic", "Standard", "Premium"). Features are what you do. Outcomes are what they get.

### Avoid the line-item dump
A proposal that lists 40 line items is a quote, not a proposal. Group line items into meaningful work packages. "Discovery and strategy" is a work package. "3 stakeholder interviews at $X each, 1 workshop at $Y, travel at cost" is a quote. Quotes invite negotiation on individual items. Proposals invite decisions on scope.

## Handling Objections Within the Proposal

Anticipate the three to five most likely objections and address them before they are raised. This reduces back-and-forth and signals that you understand the client's decision process.

### Common objections and where to address them

**"We've tried this before and it didn't work."**
Address in the Evidence section. Name what typically goes wrong with this type of engagement and explain specifically why your approach is different. Cite a client who had a similar prior failure and succeeded with your approach.

**"This is too expensive."**
Address in the Investment section. Show the cost of the current problem. Present price in context of outcome. Offer a phased option if the full scope creates budget risk.

**"We don't have the internal bandwidth to support this."**
Address in the Scope section. Be explicit about what you handle and what you need from them. If your model is low-touch on their side, say so and explain how it works.

**"We're not sure you have experience with companies like ours."**
Address in Evidence and Credentials. Lead with the most relevant case study. Describe the similarity explicitly: "Like you, this client was a Series B company scaling a distributed sales team for the first time."

**"The timeline seems aggressive."**
Address in Timeline and Milestones. Break the timeline into phases with clear milestones and show what is needed from them at each phase. Ambiguous timelines invite doubt. Milestones with owners and dates create confidence.

## Example Proposal Structures

### Example 1: B2B SaaS -- Sales Enablement Proposal

**Executive summary** (client-first)
Opens: "Your sales team is closing deals at 11% from initial meeting to signed contract -- roughly half the benchmark for companies at your stage and sales motion. Based on our discovery sessions, the gap is not in prospecting volume or product-market fit. It is in the handoff from SDR to AE and the consistency of qualification criteria across the team."

Recommended solution: a 90-day enablement engagement building a repeatable qualification framework, AE playbook, and manager coaching program.

Evidence reference: one directly comparable case study (Series B SaaS, SDR/AE split model) where conversion improved from 9% to 17% over 6 months.

**Situation and problem**
- Current state: 45-person sales team, 8 SDRs, 12 AEs, no documented qualification framework
- Problem: conversion from SQL to closed-won is 11%; industry benchmark is 18-22% for this motion
- Cost of gap: at current pipeline volume, the delta between 11% and 18% conversion represents approximately $2.4M in annual revenue at risk

**Proposed solution**
- Phase 1 (weeks 1-4): Discovery -- shadow calls, interview AEs and SDRs, audit CRM data
- Phase 2 (weeks 5-10): Build qualification framework, AE playbook, and scoring rubric; pilot with 3 AEs
- Phase 3 (weeks 11-12): Roll out to full team; train managers on coaching framework

**Evidence**
Case study: similar-stage SaaS client, same SDR/AE model, conversion improved from 9% to 17% in 6 months post-implementation.

**Investment**
Total engagement: $95,000 over 90 days. Estimated revenue impact: $1.8M-$2.4M in additional closed revenue annually at current pipeline volume.

**Next steps**
Review and sign SOW by [date]. Kickoff call scheduled for [date]. Discovery phase begins [date].

### Example 2: Professional Services -- Operations Consulting Proposal

**Executive summary**
Opens: "Your customer onboarding process is taking an average of 34 days to complete -- more than three times the benchmark for B2B SaaS companies at your scale and segment. Three of your five largest customers in the last year cited slow onboarding as a factor in their decision to reduce contract scope at renewal."

Recommended solution: a 60-day process redesign engagement to cut onboarding from 34 days to under 10.

**Situation and problem**
- 34-day average onboarding driven by 12-step manual handoff process between sales, implementation, and customer success
- 3 of 5 largest 2023 renewals reduced scope, with slow onboarding cited in exit interviews
- Estimated customer lifetime value impact: $1.1M in downgraded ARR attributable to onboarding friction

**Proposed solution**
- Map current process and identify bottlenecks (2 weeks)
- Design and pilot a streamlined 5-step onboarding flow (3 weeks)
- Build internal documentation and train CS team (2 weeks)
- Measure and report outcomes against 10-day target (ongoing, first 30 days post-launch)

**Evidence**
Three comparable client outcomes: median onboarding reduction of 68% within 90 days of implementation.

**Handling the bandwidth objection**
"This engagement requires approximately 4 hours per week from your VP of Customer Success and 2 hours per week from your implementation lead. We handle all documentation, facilitation, and process design work. Your team reviews and approves."

**Investment**
$72,000 total engagement. Phased option available: Phase 1 (diagnosis and design) for $38,000, with Phase 2 (implementation and training) contracted separately.
